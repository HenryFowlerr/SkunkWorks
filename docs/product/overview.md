# Chappe: product understanding and demo brief

Updated 26 September 2026 from Henry's spoken walkthrough of the handwritten workflow diagram and his later corrections. **Chappe** is the customer-facing product name. **SkunkWorks** is the SaaSathon team, GitHub repository, and Supabase organization name. This file is the current product brief for every new coding or design chat. It records Henry's intent; the implementation status lives in [current status](../integration/status.md). The earlier sheet-metal-only build package is historical technical context, not the current product scope.

## The problem and promise

Engineers and designers send technical drawings, CAD files, and other design information to a manufacturing facility. The people making the product can encounter details that are difficult to interpret or operations the facility cannot perform as specified. Clarifying those issues across the engineering-to-workshop boundary takes time, causes rework, and can delay a prototype. Chappe aims to preserve design intent, surface documented facility conflicts before handoff, and give the person on the floor a fast way to understand difficult work and return a precise question to engineering.

The desired experience is a **website** that judges can open from a link. It has a public business landing page, email sign-up/sign-in through Supabase, an engineer desktop area, a manufacturer desktop area, and a phone-friendly operator area opened by a QR code. The product has to support a five-minute pitch and a live demo. It does not have to automate every real manufacturing case in the SaaSathon timeframe. Demonstrated actions must be clear and reliable, while prepared example files, precomputed visual outputs, and a rehearsed path are acceptable when labelled honestly.

## Henry's diagram, from top to bottom

1. **Engineering input.** Engineers/designers have technical drawings, a 3D CAD model, and possibly ordinary drawings or other design information. They upload the information for a particular project or product into a job-specific knowledge base. For the live demo, these files may already be uploaded to save time, but the website still needs a comprehensible upload and review area.
2. **Receiving facility.** The engineer selects the manufacturing facility to which the design is being sent. That facility maintains a record of its equipment, tooling, and relevant process notes through a manufacturer desktop view. This can be mostly staged in the demo, but the selected profile must inform the check and remain visible alongside the job.
3. **Pre-handoff check.** Chappe compares explicit design requirements against the selected facility's documented capabilities. A known tooling/size/process mismatch should be shown as a conflict for review. Missing capability data stays **unknown**, not “possible.” A geometrically impossible or inaccessible operation, such as a drilling orientation that cannot be reached as described, needs engineering/fixture evidence or human review; the app must not assert that arbitrary PDF/CAD geometry has been fully physically validated. The engineer resolves blockers or changes the design/facility/setup before release.
4. **Selective visual guide proposal.** The knowledge base and AI prompt generate an illustrated, phone-readable guide **only for unusually complex parts or operations that need extra explanation**. Routine screws, drill selection, and ordinary shop know-how do not belong in a verbose manual. The AI may suggest which operations warrant a guide, but the engineer has to check the generated interpretation, edit/correct it, choose what belongs on the floor, and approve the exact version before it is sent out. No generated step silently becomes an instruction. The team's visual output design is still being prepared; do not lock the final illustration style or card format prematurely.
5. **Publication and QR.** After engineer approval, the reviewed guide is released as a version tied to the job and technical drawing. Chappe provides a small QR label to put in a corner of the drawing. Scanning with a phone's normal camera opens the released website guide. A QR on an older paper drawing should still identify the old release and make any replacement explicit.
6. **Operator phone experience.** The person doing the job needs a simple, quick interface. The phone view opens with a lightweight 3D representation of the final product where supported. They can orbit/zoom, select the area or operation they are working on, and jump directly to its short visual sequence. They should not have to swipe through hundreds of irrelevant steps. The initial codebase supports an authored straight-bend sheet-metal example with numbered bend selection; this is a technical starting point, not the chosen final demo product.
7. **Questions, voice, flags, and learning.** The QR phone surface is deliberately sparse: the released product/operation and approved instruction stay visible, with a bottom action bar for “Speak or type question” and “Flag & hold.” Browser dictation is optional, always has an editable typed transcript, and does not keep raw audio by default. A contextual assistant may only use the job's approved sources; it must state uncertainty, cannot clear a hold, and cannot invent an engineering answer. When the operator says the assist resolved the immediate uncertainty, Chappe records the raw question plus an assistant-drafted improvement candidate for the exact product/operation. This is a review note, not a change to the released guide.
8. **Fast engineer loop.** A red flag creates a hard hold first, then carries the exact job, release, operation, raw operator statement, and an assistant-drafted investigation report to the product's engineering queue. The dashboard makes the urgent flag visible ahead of ordinary improvement reports; an in-app alert is not a promise of a staffed human response time. The engineer decides whether to keep the hold, send a clarification, correct a guide step, alter the design, or publish a new reviewed release. A model-proposed report or fix is not automatically authoritative. The floor view shows only the engineer-approved response and, if needed, the replacement guide explicitly. This closes the communication loop between the people designing and the people making the product.

The diagram also depicts a manufacturer laptop/dashboard between engineering and the operator phone. Its meaningful minimum for the demo is a facility profile, relevant job handoff, and issue context. A complete enterprise production management system is outside the pitch scope.

## The three user surfaces

| Surface | Primary tasks | Demo priority |
|---|---|---|
| Public site | Explain the value and features; direct people to email sign-up/sign-in. | Real, judge-accessible website. Visual language can be refined in a separate design chat. |
| Engineer desktop | Select facility; upload/review job sources; inspect documented capability conflicts and unknowns; select and correct difficult guide steps; approve publication; review floor questions/flags and suggested responses. | Core demo. Engineer approval of the generated guide is essential. |
| Manufacturer desktop | Maintain a versioned facility/equipment profile; see the jobs sent to the workshop and documented setup needs. | Simple but coherent. Profile editing may be staged initially, while the selected data must influence the shown check. |
| Operator phone | Scan QR, inspect model/area, open only relevant difficult instructions, ask by text or voice, flag an issue, see the engineer response. | Core demo. Keep controls large, navigation direct, and the current job/release visible. |

## Demo versus future capability

The **five-minute demonstration** should follow one prepared job end to end: show the public page, sign in, open the engineer job with files already loaded, show the selected facility and a defensible capability/unknown finding, inspect one selectively proposed difficult visual guide, make or show a correction, approve it, display the QR, open the phone view, jump to the relevant operation, ask/flag a problem, and show the engineer's contextual response. This should be rehearsed with a known example and a fallback if a live AI call is slow. Prepared outputs are acceptable when they are clearly described as prepared; the UI must not claim a live check or a physically safe process that did not run.

The final example part/product is **undecided**. Do not treat the current bend demo as the product definition. The visual guide artwork/report shape, final brand design file, exact chosen model, the AI model/prompt, and hosting destination are also still to be decided with Henry and the team. Make layout and contracts flexible so those can change without rebuilding the whole site.

A credible demo check can compare cited drawing requirements to confirmed facility facts and flag missing evidence. General collision analysis, arbitrary CAD topology extraction, toolpath generation, and certification of manufacturability would require much more engineering data and validation. These are possible future capabilities, not demonstrated facts. OpenAI structured output constrains response shape; it does not prove that a proposed engineering step is correct. Human review is the authority gate.

## Current implementation boundary

The existing repository started as a straight-bend sheet-metal prototype. It includes synthetic PDF/GLB/manifest examples, a bend geometry viewer, a typed review/guide UI, and an OpenAI grounding adapter. Preserve that useful foundation while broadening the **product framing**. The current geometry renderer is an illustrative rigid-panel view, not a press-brake or physical feasibility simulator. The operator interface and engineer dashboard depend on API routes that are still being completed; see [current status](../integration/status.md) before claiming a working flow.

## Design principles for the next chat

- Lead with the actual user's decision: “Can this workshop make this?” and “What do I do at this difficult operation?”
- Keep the operator's route from QR to the selected operation very short. The model is a navigation aid, and the guide should contain only the unusual work that merits one.
- Show source and confidence next to engineering claims. Use explicit states for supported, conflict, missing, and unreadable information.
- Make the engineer's review and approval of generated material obvious. Distinguish a proposed step from a released step and a suggested fix from an approved answer.
- Use useful visual hierarchy and responsive layout now; a separate design file will define detailed brand language later.
- Never imply that an AI-generated instruction, a source citation, or a facility profile certifies physical safety or manufacturability.
